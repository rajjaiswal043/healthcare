Walk-in Diagnostics

Somebody walks in wanting an X-ray and does not want to see a doctor. Every test in the system today exists because a doctor asked for it — this is the other case.

From clinic demos, 3 Sep 2026 6 requirements 6 decisions outstanding Companion to “Finance Desk and Promotions”

What is being asked for

Today a test exists because a doctor asked for it. During a consultation the doctor raises the order, the patient walks to the X-ray room, the department does the work and attaches the result, and the result comes back into the consultation for the doctor to read. That chain assumes a doctor at both ends.

The clinics want the other case: a patient walks in off the street wanting a film. They may be carrying a prescription from a hospital across town, or they may simply want a chest X-ray. They pay, the department does the test, they take the report away. No consultation, no prescription, no doctor anywhere in it.

The flow the clinics described
  1. The patient arrives and says which test they want.
  2. Finance finds or registers them, quotes the price for that test, and takes the payment.
  3. Front desk raises the request against the department.
  4. The department performs the test, attaches the result, and hands the report over.

Requirements

FR-DIA-10

A test request with no ordering doctor

New build

A diagnostic order that stands on its own — raised because a patient asked for it, rather than because a doctor prescribed it.

  • The order records who asked for it: this clinic's doctor as today, a doctor at another hospital, or the patient themselves.
  • It behaves like any other order on the department worklist — the department does not need to care why it exists.
  • It attaches to the patient, not to a consultation, so it appears in their history for any doctor who sees them later.
For the developer

diagnostic_order currently hangs off a consultation and carries the ordering practitioner. Both become optional, and a referral source — in-house doctor, external doctor, or self — is stored explicitly rather than inferred from a null consultation. Inferring it works today and breaks the first time a fourth case appears; the reports below need the distinction stated, not deduced. Everything downstream already exists.

FR-DIA-11

Pricing and paying for a walk-in test

Extends existing

The finance desk quotes the test, takes the money and prints a receipt — the same desk and the same screen as a consultation, with a test in place of a doctor's fee.

  • Finance selects the test, not a doctor. The price comes from the clinic's priced services, which already exist.
  • Several tests on one bill — an X-ray and a blood test together — is one invoice, one payment, one receipt.
  • Promotions apply here too, if the clinic decides they do. That is an open question in the companion document.
The gate

The department does not start work until the test is paid for. Same rule as pay-first at check-in, applied to the worklist instead of the queue: an unpaid test shows on the worklist marked unpaid and cannot be started. A clinic that prices nothing is unaffected, as today.

FR-DIA-12

Raising the request at the front desk

New build

After paying, the patient goes to the front desk, which sends the request to the department with no consultation involved. Front desk picks the patient, the department and the test; it lands on that department's worklist immediately; whoever raised it is recorded.

For the developer

The front desk needs the permission to order a test, which it does not have today. This is a genuine widening of what the front desk can do — and decision 1 below asks whether it should be finance doing this instead.

FR-DIA-13

Doing the test and handing the report over

Extends existing

The department performs the test, attaches the film or result, writes its report, and gives the report to the patient — because there is no doctor waiting for it.

  • Same worklist and same result attachment as a doctor-ordered test. The department's screen does not change.
  • The report is printed and handed over, and the system records that it was handed over and when. A patient who paid and left without their film is a complaint waiting to happen.
  • The result stays on the patient record. If they see a doctor here in six months, it is in their history like any other test.
FR-DIA-14

The doctor who sent them, from somewhere else

New build

Many walk-ins arrive holding a prescription from a doctor at another hospital, and in Indian practice the report is addressed back to that doctor by name.

  • Capture the referring doctor's name, and their clinic and number if given.
  • Print it on the report.
  • Report on it. Which outside doctors send the most work is a number the owner will want — and it is the start of a referral relationship.
Scope

Start as free text on the order. A managed list of external referrers is the obvious next step and should not be built until somebody asks for it.

FR-DIA-15

Money that belongs to no doctor

Extends existing

A test-only patient never sees a doctor, so a report that splits revenue by doctor cannot see them at all.

FR-FIN-03 says collections split by doctor. It must say by doctor and by department, or walk-in test revenue disappears from the finance person's own reports. Whoever picks up either requirement should read both.

Decisions still needed

1. Should finance raise the request, instead of the front desk?

In the flow as described, finance already selects the test in order to price it, and then the front desk selects it again in order to request it. The same information is typed twice by two people, each with a patient in front of them — and the second entry can silently disagree with the first. Paid for a chest X-ray, requested a knee.

Recommendation Finance raises the request as part of taking the payment, and the front desk simply points the patient at the right room. One less step for the patient, one less place for the two records to diverge, and no new power for the front desk. Against it: more on the finance desk, and it is not what the clinics described. Your call — but make it deliberately, because building it as described means accepting the double entry.

2. Does a walk-in test patient get a token?

Consultation patients get a token at booking and appear on the waiting-room screen. A patient waiting for an X-ray is also waiting, in the same room. Token and screen, or do departments just work their list?

3. Does the printed report say no clinician has read it?

Nobody interprets a self-referred test. The clinic is selling a film and a technical report, not a diagnosis — and the patient may well read it as one. A medico-legal question for the doctors, not a technical one, but it must be settled before reports print.

4. Must a test be paid before the department starts?

Recommendation Yes, consistent with pay-first everywhere else. Worth confirming, because a clinic that lets a regular pay afterwards will find it obstructive.

5. What if they then decide they do want a doctor?

The technician sees something on the film and now the patient wants a consultation. Can that test be pulled into the consultation that follows, so the doctor reads the film they just paid for rather than ordering it again? This will happen. Better answered than discovered.

6. Free text or a managed list for external referrers?

Free text is right to start with. A managed list gives referral reporting that actually adds up, at the cost of somebody maintaining it. Start free, and watch whether anybody reads the report.
